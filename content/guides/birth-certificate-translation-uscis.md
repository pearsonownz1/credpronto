# How to translate a birth certificate for USCIS

*By Guy Pearson. Guy co-founded ImmiTranslate in 2012, later sold it, and runs GEO Credential Services (GCS.org). He has years of experience with translations and credential documents for immigration filings.*

*Last updated Oct 12, 2026*

The birth certificate is the document we see most often in immigration cases, and it gets more small mistakes than any other. Usually the problem isn't the language. It's a stamp nobody translated, a name spelled two different ways, or a certificate that doesn't show the information USCIS needs.

This guide covers birth certificates specifically: which version to get, what goes into the translation, and how to file it. For the general translation rule, see [Does USCIS require a certified translation?](/guides/uscis-certified-translation)

## Step 1: Start with the right certificate

Check that the document itself works before anyone translates it. A perfect translation of the wrong certificate won't help your case.

USCIS's Policy Manual chapter on adjustment of status documentation says an applicant must submit "a copy of the applicant's foreign birth certificate or sufficient secondary evidence of birth," and that "each foreign birth certificate must include a certified English translation." ([USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 7, Part A, Ch. 4](https://www.uscis.gov/policy-manual/volume-7-part-a-chapter-4))

The same chapter tells officers to check the State Department's country reciprocity schedule, which lists for each country whether birth certificates are available and what secondary evidence is acceptable. It also says a certification of non-existence from a civil authority "is not required where the Department of State's Reciprocity Schedule indicates this type of document generally does not exist." You can look up your country on the State Department's [Reciprocity and Civil Documents by Country](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/Visa-Reciprocity-and-Civil-Documents-by-Country.html) page. Find your country of birth and open its birth certificate section. It tells you which document to request and from which office.

### Long form vs. short form

Many countries issue more than one version of a birth record. Some short "extracts" or abstracts leave out the parents' names. Whether a short version works depends on your case and your country's entry in the reciprocity schedule, so check there first.

For one program the State Department states it outright. Its Diversity Visa instructions say: "You must submit a long form original birth certificate. Short form birth certificates will not be accepted." It lists what the certificate must contain: the person's date of birth, place of birth, names of both parents, and an "annotation by the appropriate authority indicating that it is an extract from the official records." ([State Department, Diversity Visa supporting documents](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/us-visas/immigrate/diversity-visa-program-entry/diversity-visa-if-you-are-selected/diversity-visa-prepare-supporting-documents.html)) That rule is written for Diversity Visa applicants, but it's a useful checklist. If your certificate shows those four things, it's unlikely to be the weak spot in a family-based filing.

## Step 2: Translate everything on the page

People tend to translate the boxes they care about, such as name, date, and parents, and skip the rest. Don't. A birth certificate usually has more on it than the main fields:

- **Seals and stamps.** Translate the wording inside them, for example "Civil Registry Office, District of …". If a seal can't be read, the translation should say so, such as "[illegible seal]", rather than leave it out.
- **Signatures.** Note them as "[signature]" along with the signer's printed name and title if those appear.
- **Marginal annotations.** Later notes about legitimation, name corrections, adoption, or a late registration often sit in the margin. They can matter as much as the main entry.
- **The back side.** Registration numbers, certification text, and apostilles are often on the reverse. Scan both sides even if the back looks empty.
- **Pre-printed form text.** Field labels, form numbers, and headers are part of the document too.

The translation should also follow the layout of the original closely enough that an officer can match each line to its source. A side-by-side format makes that easy.

## Step 3: Get every name spelled consistently

This is where birth certificate translations most often cause trouble. The same person can show up as "Mohammad" on a passport, "Muhammad" on a marriage certificate, and "Mohamed" in a translator's transliteration. Every variation is one more thing an officer has to work out.

A few practical rules:

- **Match the passport.** If a name already appears in Latin letters on the person's passport, use that spelling in the translation unless the certificate itself spells it differently in Latin letters.
- **Keep the original's name order.** Don't move surnames around to fit American order. Show the names the way the certificate does.
- **Don't "correct" the record.** If the certificate has a misspelling or a wrong date, the translation should reproduce it faithfully. A translator can add a short bracketed note, but fixing the record is a matter for the civil registry, not the translator.
- **Check it yourself.** You know how your family's names are spelled on your other documents, and the translator doesn't. Review every name and date against your passport and your other filings before you finalize.

## Step 4: The certification

The translator signs a statement confirming the translation is complete and accurate and that they're competent to translate from that language into English. The full wording and the rule behind it are in our [USCIS translation guide](/guides/uscis-certified-translation). For a birth certificate, make sure the statement names the document specifically, for example "birth certificate of [full name], issued by [authority]." That way the certification can't get separated from the record it covers.

USCIS doesn't require notarization for this. If a different office, such as a state agency or a foreign consulate, asks for a notarized copy of the same translation, see [Certified vs. notarized translation](/guides/certified-vs-notarized-translation).

## Step 5: Put the packet together and file it

Keep three things together for each birth certificate: a copy of the original (both sides), the English translation, and the translator's certification.

- **Filing online:** USCIS's [Tips for Filing Forms Online](https://www.uscis.gov/file-online/tips-for-filing-forms-online) say to upload the original document along with the certified English translation and the translator's certificate. Files must be clear and readable, generally PDF, JPG, or JPEG, and no larger than 12 MB. Don't password-protect them.
- **Filing by mail:** put the copy of the original, the translation, and the certification in that order so they stay together. Send a copy, not your original certificate, unless USCIS specifically asks for the original.

Keep a full set for yourself. If an officer asks about the document at an interview, you'll want the same version they have.
